## 2.1 Business Law: history & concept

**Q: When and why did Business Law arise as a special law?**

In the **Middle Ages**, after the fall of the Roman Empire, as a **customary** law of **traders** (merchants, guilds, **Consulados del Mar**).


**Q: Which Commercial Codes did Spain have?**

**1829** (first, after the French code of **1807**) and the **1885 Commerce Code**, still the framework code today – though most of its articles have been repealed and replaced by special laws.


**Q: Define **Business Law** (notes).**

The part of the **private** legal system for **entrepreneurs** and their **legal status**, dealing with their **activities in the market** carried out **through an enterprise**.


**Q: Three fundamental notes of Business Law?**

1. Part of **private law**
2. **Special** private law (vs general civil law)
3. The law of **entrepreneurs**


**Q: Are **employment law** and **tax law** part of Business Law?**

**No.** They affect entrepreneurs but are separate disciplines. Not every rule affecting entrepreneurs is Business Law.



## 2.2 Sources of Business Law

**Q: What does **art. 38 CE** say and why is it important?**

**Free enterprise** is recognised **within the framework of a market economy**; public authorities guarantee and protect it. It is the constitutional basis of trade activity and of competition law.


**Q: Who has competence over commercial legislation in Spain?**

The **State, exclusively** (art. **149.1.6 CE**) – to guarantee **market unity**.


**Q: In what order are **acts of commerce** governed (art. 2 CCom)?**

1. **Commerce Code** (and laws replacing its provisions)
2. **Business practice/usage** of each place
3. **Ordinary civil law**

> Unlike in civil law, **business custom comes before civil law** – a trace of Business Law's customary origin.


**Q: What limitation does **art. 50 CCom** add?**

For **business contracts** without a specific law, **general civil law** applies directly – business usage does **not** come first. Important limit because contracts are exactly where customs matter most.


**Q: What is the **lex specialis** principle?**

**Specialised acts prevail over general acts.** It explains the many special commercial laws (LSC, Trademarks Act, LDC…) next to the old CCom.



## 2.3 Enterprise vs entrepreneur

**Q: Define **enterprise** (empresa). Does it have legal personality?**

An **organised set of capital** (material & immaterial elements) **and work** (people) used to produce/distribute goods or services for the market. It has **no legal personality** – it must be linked to a person: the **entrepreneur**.

> When people say "the enterprise signed a contract" they are speaking loosely – legally it is the **entrepreneur** (the owner) who holds rights and obligations.


**Q: Define **entrepreneur** in the legal sense.**

The **natural or legal person** that, **in its own name** and in an **organised and professional** manner, carries out an economic activity of producing/distributing goods or providing services **for the market**, assuming the **responsibilities** and holding the **rights** arising from it.

> Legal sense ≠ economic sense. Economically the "entrepreneur" is whoever makes decisions (directors, managers). Legally it is **in whose name** the activity is run.


**Q: Three defining features of the legal concept of entrepreneur?**

1. **Organisational** activity
2. **Professional** activity (habitual, public, for the market, usually for profit)
3. Acts **in its own name**

> Memory trick **O-P-O**: Organises, Professional, Own name.


**Q: Why is "buying an **enterprise**" legally wrong? What do you buy?**

You buy a **business / business establishment** (negocio, establecimiento mercantil): the assets **plus** pre-existing legal and financial relationships (contracts, employees, reputation, brand). An enterprise as such is just a set of assets/people.

> Mock exam Q1: "enterprise", "entrepreneur" and "business/commercial establishment" are **different concepts** → answer **(b)**.


**Q: Who are businesspersons under **art. 1 CCom**?**

1. Those with legal capacity who engage in commerce **habitually**
2. **Business or industrial companies** incorporated under the Code


**Q: Are **directors, attorneys or employees** entrepreneurs?**

**No** – they act **in the name and on behalf of another** (the entrepreneur), don't assume the obligations and don't receive the profits.



## 2.4 Types of entrepreneurs

**Q: Three classifications of entrepreneurs?**

1. **Natural** (individual / sole trader) vs **legal** person (business entity)
2. By **size**: small, medium, large
3. **Private** vs **public**


**Q: Does the size of the enterprise change the legal status?**

**No** – the CCom gives one status regardless of size. But special rules differ, e.g. **accounting** (General Accounting Plan vs SME plan; art. 25 CCom) and corporate tax for small companies. SME parameters: **Commission Regulation (EU) 651/2014, Annex I**.



## 2.5 Legal status of the entrepreneur

**Q: What does the **legal status of the entrepreneur** include?**

1. **Professional duties**
2. **Rights**
3. **Legal capacity**
4. **Liability regime**


**Q: Three **professional duties** of every entrepreneur?**

1. Keep **accounts** and documentation (art. **25** CCom)
2. **Legal publicity** – registration in the **Mercantile Registry** (mandatory for companies, **optional for individual entrepreneurs**)
3. Duty to respect **free and fair competition** (LDC + Unfair Competition Act)

> Trap: registration is **not** mandatory for sole traders.


**Q: Double purpose of registering in the Mercantile Registry?**

1. **Presumption of legality** of registered elements (filter of lawfulness)
2. **Market transparency** – publicity of sensitive information for third parties


**Q: Rights given by the status of entrepreneur?**

1. **Participate in the market** competing freely
2. **Demand fair competition** (stop abuse/restrictive practices)
3. **Exclusive use** of distinctive signs (trademarks, trade names) and **exclusive exploitation** of industrial creations (patents)


**Q: **Legal capacity** vs **capacity to operate (act)**?**

**Legal capacity**: ability to **hold rights and obligations** – from **birth**, for life.
**Capacity to operate**: ability to perform acts with legal effect – requires **legal age (18)** and **not being judicially incapacitated**.

> Baby example: a newborn can **own** a bank account (legal capacity) but cannot **dispose** of it (no capacity to operate). Mock Q6 → (a).


**Q: Who can be an **individual entrepreneur** (art. 4 CCom)?**

Those of **legal age** with **free disposal of their assets**.


**Q: Two exceptions where minors/incapacitated persons can be entrepreneurs?**

1. A **minor or incapacitated heir** continuing the business of their **parents/testators**
2. An entrepreneur who **becomes incapacitated** continuing his/her own previous business
→ in both cases they act **through legal representatives**.

> Justified by the principle of **preserving the enterprise**.


**Q: **Incompatibilities (conflicts of interest)** – absolute vs relative?**

Restrictions on people with public functions to protect impartiality.
**Absolute**: no business anywhere in Spain (members of Government, senior management of agencies).
**Relative**: only in the territory where they serve (**judges, prosecutors**).


**Q: Three legal **prohibitions to compete**?**

1. **Agents/managers/representatives** vs their principal (art. **288 CCom**)
2. **Workers** vs their employer (Workers' Statute)
3. **Directors** vs their company (LSC) – unless **express consent** of the company

> Breach → removal/dismissal + damages, **but contracts concluded remain valid** vs bona fide third parties.


**Q: What is a **disqualification** (inhabilitación)?**

Prevention of business activity **as a sanction** for irregularities; can be **temporary or perpetual**. Some activities also need **prior administrative authorisation**.



## 2.6 Liability regime

**Q: Three questions of the entrepreneur's liability?**

1. **For what facts?** Acts/omissions causing damage through fault or negligence – **contractual and non-contractual**
2. **For whom?** Own acts **and** those of dependants (employees, representatives)
3. **With what?** **All present and future assets** (art. **1911 CC**)

> Mock Q5 → (a): liable even for damages caused by workers in the exercise of their job.


**Q: Individual entrepreneur: is there a separation between business and private assets?**

As a rule **no** – liable with **all present and future assets**, business and private. (New laws tend to protect the family home.)

> That's why sole traders often form an **S.L.U.** – a single-member company limits liability to the company's assets.



## 2.7 Business entities

**Q: What is a trading company (art. 116 CCom) and when does it get legal personality?**

A contract by which **two or more persons** put goods/industry into a **common fund to obtain profit**. Once incorporated per the Code it has **legal personality** in all its acts and contracts.


**Q: Three effects of legal personality of a company?**

1. Subject of **rights and obligations** (full capacity externally and internally)
2. Acquires **business status** (subject to its articles)
3. **Owns its assets** separate from the members → separation of liability


**Q: Why does a company need natural persons to act?**

"It has no hands to sign nor a brain to decide" → **representatives**: **legal** (scope set by law, e.g. directors) or **voluntary** (powers of attorney granted at will).


**Q: With what does a business entity answer for its debts?**

**Always with all its assets** (art. 1911 CC), whatever its type. *Whether members also answer* depends on the type: yes in partnerships, no in capital companies.


**Q: What is an **irregular company** and what liability does it face?**

A company operating **without registration** in the Mercantile Registry. Managers are **jointly and severally liable** to third parties (art. **120 CCom**) and members answer like a **general partnership** (personally) – **even if it is a capital company**.

> Internal liability agreements between members have no effect against third parties.


**Q: Four basic types of trading companies (art. 122 CCom)? Closed list?**

1. **General partnership** (S. colectiva)
2. **Limited partnership** (S. comanditaria)
3. **Public limited company** (S.A.)
4. **Private limited company** (S.R.L./S.L.)
Not closed – "**as a general rule**" → the law may create new types.


**Q: Which are **capital companies** (art. 1 LSC)?**

**S.A.** (public limited), **S.R.L./S.L.** (private limited) and the **sociedad comanditaria por acciones** (partnership limited by shares). Regulated by the **LSC – RLD 1/2010**.


**Q: Most important difference: partnerships vs capital companies?**

The **liability of members**. Partnership members: **personal, unlimited, subsidiary and joint & several**. Capital company members: **not personally liable** for company debts (art. 1 LSC).

> Mock Q8 → (b): members are liable for company debts **only in personalistic companies (partnerships)**.


**Q: What do **personal, unlimited, subsidiary, joint & several** mean for a general partner?**

- **Personal**: with own assets
- **Unlimited**: for all debts, present and future assets
- **Subsidiary**: only if the partnership can't pay
- **Joint & several**: creditor can claim **the whole** from any partner, who can later claim the others' shares


**Q: What is **lifting the corporate veil**?**

When members **abuse** the legal personality (mixing personal and company assets, acting as if the company didn't exist), courts **disregard the separation** and hold them **personally liable** (arts. **6.4 and 7 CC** – abuse of rights/fraud).


**Q: General partnership: 3 characteristics + name rules?**

1. Partners **liable** (personal, unlimited, subsidiary, joint & several)
2. **Personalistic** – trust; no transfer of partner status without consent (art. 143 CCom)
3. Business **name and one signature**: name of all/some partners (+ "y Cía.") + "S.C."/"S.R.C.". A non-partner whose name appears is **liable as a partner** (art. 126 CCom).


**Q: Limited partnership: two kinds of partners?**

**General partners**: unlimited liability, **manage**, names in business name.
**Limited partners**: liable only **up to their contribution**, **no management**, **name not allowed** in the business name, can transfer status **without consent**. Abbr. **S.Com. / S. en C.**


**Q: Limited partnership vs partnership limited by shares?**

Limited partnership: capital in **stakes**, CCom. Partnership limited by **shares**: capital in **shares** (securities) → treated as a **capital company** (LSC). Both have general + limited partners.


**Q: Define the **S.A.** (public limited company).**

Company of **capitalist character**, capital divided into **shares**, made up of members' contributions, members **not personally liable** for company debts.


**Q: Define the **S.R.L./S.L.** (private limited company). Why "hybrid"?**

Capitalist company, capital divided into **stakes (participaciones)**, members not personally liable. **Hybrid**: fully capitalist towards **third parties**, but **internally flexible/personalistic** – e.g. by-laws can give a minority member the majority of votes (impossible in an S.A.).

> S.L. is the **most used** company in Spain (lower minimum capital + flexibility).
