## 1.1 Law: objective vs subjective

**Q: What is law in the **objective** sense?**

The **organised set of rules** made by those with power to do so in a society, which can be **imposed coercively** (e.g. "Spanish law").

> Think of objective law as *the rule book* of a country. Every rule is part of a structured whole = the **legal system**.


**Q: What is law in the **subjective** sense?**

The **power of a person** to do, demand or prohibit something, **protected by objective law**, with the possibility of going to court ("I have the right to…").

> Subjective law only exists because objective law recognises it. Objective = the rule book; subjective = *your* right that comes out of it.



## 1.2 The legal rule: characters

**Q: What are the **two characters** of a legal rule?**

1. **Generality**
2. **Coercibility** (coercive / enforceable character)

> Mnemonic: a legal rule is **G**eneral and **C**an be forced – **GC**.


**Q: What does **generality** of a legal rule mean? Give the example from the notes.**

Rules address **abstract categories**, not individual cases. A new formal obligation for *all* public limited companies = legal rule. A decree **appointing one person** to a public office = **not** a legal rule (no generality).

> Ask yourself: does it target a *type* of person/situation or *one specific* person? Only the first is a legal rule.


**Q: What does **coercibility** mean? Why is saying "good morning" in class not a legal rule?**

If a rule is not obeyed voluntarily, compliance can be **imposed by institutionalised force** (power given to the **courts**). Greeting someone is a **social rule** – nobody can force you by institutional force.

> Social norms, manners and educational rules regulate coexistence too, but they lack institutionalised enforcement.


**Q: Which three elements make sure legal rules are applied effectively?**

1. **Interpretation**
2. **Validity** (birth → death of the rule)
3. **Effectiveness** (ignorance of the law is no excuse)


**Q: How must rules be **interpreted** (art. 3 CC)?**

According to the **proper meaning of their words**, in connection with the **context**, their **historical and legislative background** and the **social reality** of the time when applied, attending to their **spirit and purpose**.

> If the literal meaning is not enough, the usual next step is the **purposive approach**: what did the legislator want to achieve with the rule?


**Q: What is the **purposive approach** to interpretation?**

Interpreting a rule according to its **spirit and purpose** – the reason why the legislator included it – when the literal interpretation is insufficient.


**Q: When is a Spanish state rule **enacted (promulgated)** and when does it **enter into force**?**

Enacted by publication in the **BOE** (Boletín Oficial del Estado). It enters into force **20 days after publication**, unless the rule itself shortens or lengthens that period.

> Publication guarantees **legal certainty**: since it's in the BOE, nobody can claim not to know it.


**Q: What is the **vacatio legis**? Example?**

The period between **publication** and **entry into force**. Example: RDL 8/2019 (employers must record working hours) had a **two-month** vacatio legis to give companies time to adapt.


**Q: Express vs tacit **repeal**?**

**Express**: the new rule explicitly derogates the old one.
**Tacit**: a new rule (same or **higher** rank) regulates the same matter differently → the old rule is invalid in everything inconsistent, even without saying so.


**Q: State the **principle of effectiveness** (art. 6 CC).**

"**Ignorance of the law does not excuse from compliance** thereof." Rules bind whether or not people know them, because they were published.

> Practical consequence: entrepreneurs should get advice from lawyers/consultants – not knowing a tax or employment rule is never a defence.



## 1.3 Mandatory vs dispositive rules

**Q: **Mandatory** vs **dispositive** rules?**

**Mandatory**: impose or prohibit conduct **absolutely**, even against the will of the parties. Acts against them are **void** (art. 6.3 CC).
**Dispositive**: apply **only if the parties do not agree otherwise** (supplementary).

> DE: zwingendes Recht vs dispositives (abdingbares) Recht.


**Q: Art. 314 CCom: loans do not accrue interest unless agreed in writing. Mandatory or dispositive?**

**Dispositive** – parties may agree interest; only if there is **no written agreement** does the default (no interest) apply.



## 1.4 Sources of law

**Q: **Material** vs **formal** sources of law?**

**Material**: the **body with power to enact** rules (State, Autonomous Communities, EU institutions…).
**Formal**: the **forms** the rules take – art. 1 CC: **law, custom, general principles of law**.


**Q: What are the formal sources of Spanish law and in which **order** do courts apply them (art. 1 CC)?**

1. **Law** (statutes)
2. **Custom** – only if there is no applicable law, if not contrary to morals/public order, and if **proven**
3. **General principles of law** – if neither law nor custom

> Mnemonic **L-C-P**: Law → Custom → Principles.


**Q: Is **case law** a source of law in Spain?**

**No.** Judges don't make rules. But the doctrine **repeatedly** upheld by the **Supreme Court** **complements** the legal system (art. 1.6 CC).

> Classic MC trap: "case law is the 4th source of law" → false. It only *complements*.


**Q: What is **custom**?**

A source of law of **spontaneous creation** by social groups through their practices. Historically very important (Business Law was born customary), today less so because legislators regulate almost everything.


**Q: Where are most **general principles of law** found? Give examples.**

In the **Constitution**: justice, freedom, equality… They resolve cases with **no law and no custom** and also shape the whole system.



## 1.5 Hierarchy of statutes

**Q: State the **principle of hierarchy** (art. 9.3 CE).**

A rule of **lower rank** may not contradict, invalidate or repeal a rule of **higher rank** – the opposite is possible.


**Q: List the Spanish **hierarchy pyramid** from top to bottom.**

1. **Constitution**
2. **International treaties; EU law**
3. **Acts** (ordinary & organic)
4. **Royal Legislative Decrees; Royal Decree-Laws**
5. **Royal Decrees** (regulations)
6. Lower rules (ministerial orders…)

> Note: RDL and RLD are made by the Government but have the **status of an act** (same rank in practice). The notes' pyramid draws them just under acts.


**Q: What is an **act** (ley) in the technical sense? Who enacts it?**

A rule enacted by the body with **maximum legislative power**: Parliament = **Cortes Generales** (Congress + Senate).


**Q: **Royal Decree-Law** vs **Royal Legislative Decree**?**

Both are made by the **Government** with the **status of an act**.
- **RDL**: for **extraordinary and urgent need**.
- **RLD**: by **express delegation** of Parliament through a **framework law** (e.g. the LSC is RLD 1/2010).

> Why delegate? Urgency (Parliament is slow) or technical complexity.


**Q: What is a **Royal Decree** and what happens if it contradicts an act?**

A rule from the **Government's own power** (not delegated), below acts/RDL/RLD – often a **regulation** developing an act (e.g. IRPF Regulation). If it contradicts an act it is **null and void**.



## 1.6 State, regions, international & EU law

**Q: Is the relationship between **State law** and **Autonomous Communities' law** one of hierarchy?**

**No – it is one of competence.** Arts. **148 and 149 CE** + each **Statute of Autonomy** distribute the matters. Regional laws are published in their own gazettes (DOGC, BOCM…).

> Mock exam Q7(a) says State rules always rank above regional rules → **false**.


**Q: Where do **international treaties** sit and what if one contradicts the Constitution (art. 95 CE)?**

Above all internal statutes, **below the Constitution**. A treaty contrary to the Constitution requires **prior constitutional amendment**.


**Q: What is EU **primary** legislation?**

The **founding Treaties** – the "rules of the game" (similar to a constitution). **Directly applicable**.


**Q: What are the two main types of EU **secondary** legislation?**

**Regulations** and **Directives**.


**Q: What is an **EU Regulation**? Example?**

Secondary law **directly applicable** to all citizens of Member States as if it were internal law – **no transposition** needed. Example: **GDPR** (Reg. 2016/679), which replaced the national data-protection rules.

> Don't confuse an **EU Regulation** (like a European act) with a Spanish **regulation** (a Royal Decree below an act).


**Q: What is an **EU Directive**?**

Secondary law addressed to **Member States** to **harmonise** their laws; States must **implement (transpose)** it into national law. In principle it binds citizens only after transposition – but the Supreme Court (STS 428/2007) says national law must be **interpreted in accordance with EU law** anyway.

> Mock exam Q2: "Directives are secondary EU provisions that standardise Member States' laws" → **correct statement (d)**.


**Q: National law vs EU law: competence or hierarchy?**

In principle **competence** (the EU legislates where States gave it competence), but in case of **conflict EU law prevails**. Spain is in the EU since **1986**.
